What is i'rāb? Arabic sentence analysis explained

By The Muhit team · 7 October 2026

What does i'rāb mean?

I'rāb (إِعْرَاب) is the change at the end of a word that shows its role in the sentence, and, by extension, the practice of explaining each word's role and ending. Students of Arabic grammar are often asked to "do the i'rāb" of a sentence.

What are the four i'rāb states?

  • الرَّفْع raf': usually shown by a ḍamma, as for the subject.
  • النَّصْب naṣb: usually shown by a fatḥa, as for the object.
  • الجَرّ jarr: usually shown by a kasra, after a preposition or in a possessive construction. Only nouns take it.
  • الجَزْم jazm: usually shown by a sukūn. Only present-tense verbs take it.

Example: جَاءَ الطَّالِبُ

  • جَاءَ: فِعْلٌ مَاضٍ مَبْنِيٌّ عَلَى الفَتْحِ, a past-tense verb, its ending fixed on fatḥa.
  • الطَّالِبُ: فَاعِلٌ مَرْفُوعٌ وَعَلَامَةُ رَفْعِهِ الضَّمَّةُ الظَّاهِرَةُ عَلَى آخِرِهِ, the subject, in raf', shown by the ḍamma on its last letter.

Example: قَرَأَ الطَّالِبُ الكِتَابَ

الكِتَابَ is the object (مَفْعُولٌ بِهِ), so it is in naṣb, shown by the fatḥa on its last letter.

What is the difference between مبني and معرب?

A declinable word (مُعْرَب) changes its ending with its role, like الطَّالِب. A built word (مَبْنِيّ) keeps the same ending whatever its role, like the past-tense verb, the pronouns and هٰذَا. Built words still have a position in the sentence; grammarians describe it as "in the place of" raf', naṣb or jarr.
